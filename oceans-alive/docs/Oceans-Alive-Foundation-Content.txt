Our Story

"We believe the ocean's future depends on the communities whose livelihoods depend on it."

Oceans Alive Foundation is an action-based Kenyan not-for-profit trust. Its aim is to create, promote, facilitate, and accelerate environmental, economic, and socio-cultural change for good on the Kenyan coast — with communities, for communities.

Our story begins in 2003 when founder Desmond Bowden responded to a call from fishermen whose livelihoods were collapsing due to coral bleaching, overfishing, and pollution. Together, they created Kenya's first community-led marine sanctuary at Kuruwitu — just 30 hectares. That sanctuary, at the fishermen's own request, became permanent.

In January 2022, Oceans Alive delivered the first Co-Management Plan in Kenya under new government guidelines — placing 150 km² of ocean under community-led management. What began as 30 hectares is now a 120 km² globally recognised model.

Founded 2003 — By Desmond Bowden & the Kuruwitu fishing community. Now operating across the full Kenyan coastline and recognised internationally.

Award: UNDP Equator Prize — Globally recognised for community-led conservation excellence.

OUR 14 PROGRAMMES

Fourteen interlocking programmes spanning coral restoration, community governance, ocean education, women's livelihoods and marine research — forming an integrated model for coastal conservation that centres the people who depend on the sea.

"Conservation that works begins and ends with the communities who call the ocean home."

At Oceans Alive Foundation, no programme stands alone. Our fourteen initiatives are deliberately interlocked — reef restoration that trains community divers, governance structures that carry fisher voices to national policy, women's livelihoods that reduce pressure on fish stocks, and youth education that plants the next generation of reef guardians.

This is the tengefu model: the Swahili concept of a protected space that belongs to everyone. When communities own their conservation, they defend it. When it provides livelihoods, they sustain it. When it educates their children, they pass it on.

FEATURED STORY
24,800 Coral Colonies and Counting: Our Reef Restoration Journey

Category: Coral Restoration
Date: March 2025
Read time: 8 minutes
Author: Oceans Alive Team, Kilifi, Kenya
Location: Kuruwitu Bay, Kilifi County

________________________________________________________________

The water is warm and startlingly clear. Thirty feet below the surface of Kuruwitu Bay, a diver moves with practised precision — placing a small coral fragment, no larger than a human hand, onto a specially prepared underwater frame. It is one act in a patient, painstaking, community-led restoration of an entire reef ecosystem. And it is one of over 24,800 such acts since 2019.

Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they sustain more than a quarter of all marine species. On Kenya's 640-kilometre coastline, these ecosystems are the foundation of life — for the ocean, for fisheries, and for the coastal communities that depend on both. When they degrade, everything follows.

At Kuruwitu Bay in Kilifi County, Oceans Alive Foundation has spent over two decades working to reverse that collapse. The coral restoration programme, launched in 2019 with us and partners Coral Guardian and Coral Reef Care, is one of the most ambitious community-led reef restoration efforts in East Africa.

24,800   Coral Colonies Hand-Planted
85%       Survival Rate on the Reef
6         Years of Active Reef Gardening


The Origins

The story of coral restoration at Kuruwitu begins not with scientists or NGOs, but with fishermen. In 2003, founder Desmond Bowden responded to a call from the Kuruwitu fishing community, whose livelihoods were visibly collapsing. Fish stocks were declining. The reef was bleaching. Families were struggling.

Together, they established Kenya's first coral-based Locally Managed Marine Area — a 30-hectare tengefu (no-take zone) that, at the fishermen's own request, became permanent. The community saw recovery almost immediately: within years, fish biomass within the tengefu began to increase. The case for protection had been made, from within the community itself.

By 2019, the success of the sanctuary created a new opportunity — and a new challenge. If the protected area had given the reef breathing room, active restoration could accelerate recovery beyond what natural regeneration alone could achieve. Oceans Alive began exploring coral gardening methodologies.


The Reef Gardeners

The most striking thing about Oceans Alive's coral programme is not the number of corals planted, nor even the survival rates. It is who does the planting.

Every diver in the programme is a member of the local Kuruwitu community. Many were fishermen before the programme began. They are now among the most skilled coral reef gardeners in Kenya — capable of identifying individual coral species, monitoring growth rates, detecting early signs of bleaching stress, and guiding visitors through the living underwater nurseries they have built.

"We used to take from the ocean. Now we give back to it. The reef is our future — if we don't care for it, there is no future for our children here."
— Kuruwitu reef gardener and community diver

Training is continuous and rigorous. Divers learn coral fragment collection, fragmentation technique, nursery management, reef survey methodology, and outplanting — the final stage of placing grown corals from the nursery onto the reef substrate. Each phase takes months to master, and the learning never truly stops.

The programme creates stable, skilled employment for community members who might otherwise rely entirely on fisheries under increasing pressure. Reef gardening and fishing — once at odds — become complementary livelihoods in a community that has staked its future on the health of the reef.

Community-First Approach

All coral restoration divers are employed from the local Kuruwitu community. Training is provided with us and partners through Coral Guardian's international methodology, adapted to local reef conditions in Kilifi County. Local knowledge — including traditional fishermen's understanding of tidal patterns, species behaviour, and seasonal changes — is embedded into the restoration programme design.


The Science

The methodology draws on coral gardening techniques developed by Coral Guardian, refined and adapted to the specific conditions of Kuruwitu Bay. The process begins in the nursery: coral fragments are collected from donor colonies, attached to specially designed underwater frames anchored in the bay at optimal depth and current conditions, and allowed to grow.

Not all coral species are equal candidates for restoration. In Kuruwitu, the programme focuses primarily on branching corals — Acropora and Pocillopora species — which tend to grow fastest, provide the most complex habitat structure, and have shown strong resilience to local temperature fluctuations. Massive corals, slower-growing but longer-lived, are also incorporated for structural diversity.

The programme has documented an average survival rate of approximately 85% from nursery to reef. Corals that survive outplanting are monitored for a minimum of twelve months, with observations logged in a data system shared with international partners.

The Four-Stage Restoration Process

1. Fragment Collection
Coral fragments are carefully collected from healthy donor colonies within the protected area, using techniques that minimise impact to the source colony.

2. Nursery Phase
Fragments are attached to underwater frames at optimal depth. Growth is monitored monthly over 6–12 months until fragments reach outplanting size.

3. Outplanting
Grown corals are carefully removed from frames and attached to prepared substrate on the degraded reef sections targeted for restoration.

4. Monitoring & Follow-up
Planted corals are monitored for survival, growth, and ecological recovery. Bleaching events, disease, and predation are tracked and responded to.


The Impact

The numbers tell part of the story. Over 24,800 coral colonies hand-planted. An 85% survival rate. Six years of continuous reef gardening. These are remarkable outcomes for a community-led programme operating in a region where coral ecosystems remain under significant pressure from climate change, pollution, and overfishing.

But the impact extends far beyond the reef itself. The presence of a thriving, growing restoration programme has transformed how the Kuruwitu community relates to the ocean. Fishermen who once measured success by their catch now measure it by the health of the reef. Children who grew up watching adults extract from the ocean are now growing up watching adults restore it.

"The reef is not just recovering — it is growing. We see fish we haven't seen in twenty years. The ocean is answering."
— Marine biologist, Kuruwitu reef survey team

Ecological surveys within the co-managed area document a 40% increase in fish biomass since formal protection began. Coral cover in restoration zones has increased measurably. Species not recorded in the area for years have been observed returning — indicators of ecosystem recovery that go beyond what coral planting alone could achieve.

The programme has also generated international visibility that supports the broader mission. Researchers, journalists, filmmakers, and conservation practitioners from across Africa and beyond have visited Kuruwitu to learn from the model — multiplying its impact far beyond a single bay on the Kenyan coast.


The Future

Fifteen thousand corals is a remarkable milestone. But the ambition of the Oceans Alive reef restoration programme extends far beyond Kuruwitu Bay. The model — community ownership, scientific rigour, long-term monitoring, integration with livelihoods — is designed to be replicated.

Plans are in development to expand the programme to additional community-managed areas along Kenya's coast, building on the Kuruwitu model and the expertise of the reef gardeners who pioneered it. Partnerships with international research institutions are deepening the scientific understanding of which restoration techniques work best under East African reef conditions.

Most importantly, the next generation of reef gardeners is already being trained. In the ocean literacy programmes running through Kilifi County schools, children as young as eight are learning about coral anatomy, reef ecology, and the methods their community uses to restore what was lost. The future of Kenya's reefs is being shaped, one coral fragment at a time, by the communities who depend on them.

The story of 24,800 coral colonies is ultimately a story about what happens when communities are trusted, equipped, and empowered to lead. Not as beneficiaries of conservation, but as its architects. The reef is recovering because the people who live alongside it decided it would — and then did the work, day after day, dive after dive, colony by colony.

That is the Oceans Alive model. And it is working.

Why It Works — The Tengefu Model

"Conservation that works begins and ends with the communities who call the ocean home."

In Kilifi, the word tengefu describes a protected space that the whole community guards. Our programme design starts from that principle: when conservation is imposed from outside, it fails. When it grows from within, it endures.

Across fourteen programmes, you will find the same pattern: community knowledge at the centre, external expertise in service of local goals, and every activity designed so that the people of Kilifi are the primary beneficiaries of a healthy ocean.

Principle 1 — Community Ownership
Every programme is co-designed with fishing communities, BMUs and local leaders. Ownership drives the accountability that makes outcomes last.

Principle 2 — Integrated Impact
Restoring a reef only works if the community has other income sources. Teaching children only works if their parents have secure livelihoods. Our programmes are deliberately interconnected.

Principle 3 — Evidence-Led Action
Research and monitoring underpin every programme. Data collected by community reef monitors informs national policy — closing the loop between science and governance.

Principle 4 — Gender at the Core
Women are agents of conservation, not beneficiaries. LIFT Network, Trash4Cash and kitchen gardens are designed to expand women's economic independence alongside ocean stewardship.
